# Frameworks — the strategic decision models

The thinking behind the copy. These are the positioning decisions made at discovery/brief time — *before* a word of the homepage is written. When an audit reveals a strategic failure (wrong buyer, no wedge, wrong market), the fix lives here, not in [exemplars.md](exemplars.md).

Distilled from a body of B2B positioning principles. Use them to reason and to answer questions; each is a lens, not a law. Most of these are ways of choosing and using the **anchors** in [anchors.md](anchors.md) — read that first; it's the grammar these strategies operate on.

## 1. The maturity fork — position in a category, or against a workflow

**The single biggest positioning decision.** Every prospect's mind already holds *shelves* (known product categories) and *slots* for jobs/workflows they're trying to get done. You can fight for a spot on an existing shelf, or attach to a job that already occupies mental space. Messaging, GTM, and pricing for the two are completely different — speaking to one alienates the other.

- **Mature market (position in a category):** buyers have budget, know the players, have bought this before. + little education needed, faster sales cycle, market certainly exists, dedicated budget. − more competition, price anchoring, risk of negative category associations, late to distribution.
- **Immature market (position against a workflow/JTBD):** a group with a job they do via tasks, workflows, or initiatives. + less vendor competition, less price anchoring, no bad associations, distribution head start. − you pay to educate, slower cycles, no budget line, the market may never materialize.

**Decision tree (to choose):**
1. Is there a category that's a natural fit? If not → does *part* of the product fit a smaller category (would you unbundle to sell it)?
2. Can people adopt just that part without the rest (trojan horse), then be upsold?
3. Would you change product, packaging, or distribution to make the fit possible?
4. Is that category's market large enough to hit your growth goals?
5. Do you have differentiation defensible for 12–18 months? Do you believe the category will grow, and do you have runway to wait?
→ endpoints: *position in a category* or *position for a workflow*.

The trade-offs are **mirror images** — each side's pros are the other's cons:

| | Category (mature) | Workflow / use case (immature) |
|---|---|---|
| Education needed | little | you pay to educate |
| Sales cycle | faster | slower |
| Market exists? | certain | may never materialize |
| Budget | dedicated line exists | no budget line to win |
| Competition | more, direct | little vendor competition |
| Price anchoring | more | less (you set the price) |
| Associations | risk of negative baggage | clean slate |
| Distribution | late to it | head start |

**Corollary (when to ignore competitors):** in an *emerging* market, looking identical to same-stage rivals is fine — it's a distribution land-grab; every dollar fighting a peer is a dollar not spent capturing greenfield. In a *mature* market with entrenched incumbents, differentiation vs. the leaders is priority #1. **Market maturity dictates positioning strategy more than anything else.**

**The naming cascade — "what you call your software determines your competitors."** The same product (Airtable) can be a project-management tool (→ Asana, ClickUp), a database (→ Postgres, MySQL), a social planner (→ Hootsuite), a no-code builder (→ Bubble). The category label you pick propagates all the way down: **category → competitor set → market/buyer → channels & tactics**. Each category is a different buyer with different needs *and explicit non-needs* — and no single "Frankenstein buyer" wants all of them at once. So you can't win several at once; **pick one beachhead to win first.** (This is the demand-capture, GTM-reaching corollary of the shelf you choose — see the retail-shelf teaching device in [exemplars.md](exemplars.md).)

## 2. Your real competition is the status quo

In almost every immature market you're not fighting rival startups — you're fighting how the buyer does the job *today*: spreadsheets, folders, manual processes, ChatGPT, an agency, or doing nothing. The founder's competitor list (other funded startups) is the wrong list; the customer doesn't know those startups exist. Leading with "we're the best [new category]" is meaningless to someone who's never had the category — like selling a smartphone to someone who's never had a landline.

**The move:** replace *"We're the best [new category]"* with *"Stop [painful status-quo way of doing the job]."* Name the manual habit as the competitor. (Vanta didn't beat other SOC-2 startups; it beat "managing your SOC 2 in spreadsheets.")

## 3. The JTBD positioning map

Most markets have no single clear competitor — just a fragmented set of alternatives all solving the same job. Build a table:

| Job-to-be-done: *[what the buyer is trying to accomplish]* | | |
|---|---|---|
| **Alternative** | **Its weakness** | **Your differentiation vs. it** |
| In-house / DIY | biased, risk of no decision | proven frameworks, done faster |
| ChatGPT | only as good as the inputs; generic | hands-on experience with every case |
| Hire a full-time role | slow to ramp, expensive | fixed scope, immediate |
| An agency (bundled) | bloated scope, upsell incentive | unbiased, one clear deliverable |
| A direct competitor | *[specific gap]* | *[specific edge]* |

**Key insight:** your differentiation *changes completely* depending on which alternative you're compared against. In marketing you get ~10 seconds, so **pick ONE primary alternative to position against**; save the multi-alternative nuance for the 30-minute sales conversation.

## 4. The demand-stage message map

A good argument in the wrong context is a bad argument — usually a timing/segmentation problem, not a bad message. Your TAM splits into three awareness stages, each needing a different message, alternative, and goal. Don't collapse the whole funnel into one narrative delivered in one sitting.

| Segment | Lead with | Message against | Main argument | Goal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Out-of-market** (no use case yet) | the outcome / business goal | the status quo | "there's a better way to *[reach outcome]*" | create market demand |
| **In-market, not shopping** (has the use case) | the use case / activity | homegrown / stitched-together tools | "there's a better way to *[do the activity]*" | create product demand |
| **In-market & shopping** (evaluating vendors) | the product category | the other vendors | "we're the best *[category]*" | capture demand |

Each message has three slots: **lead with / message against / main argument.** Two classic mistakes: wrong message to wrong stage; and trying to create *and* capture demand at once.

## 5. Problem ownership — the 2×2

In early-stage B2B, what matters isn't how big or painful the problem is — it's whether a *specific person* owns it, feels it, and can act on it. The broader the pain, the more it fragments across departments and the more it needs C-level sponsorship that rarely comes.

- **X-axis:** small problem (no one cares) → big problem (people suffer)
- **Y-axis:** no clear owner (only C-suite can authorize) → clear owner (someone below C-suite can authorize)

| | Small | Big |
|---|---|---|
| **Clear owner** | Tough but doable | **Sweet spot** ← aim here |
| **No owner** | Tumbleweed country | Danger zone (where startups die) |

**Diagnostic:** Who owns solving this? Is it anyone's job? Is anyone actively looking? Does anyone have budget? If a vivid, company-wide problem has no owner, it's the danger zone — "everyone's problem" is no one's.

## 6. ICP by use-case, not firmographics

Most companies define their ICP as firmographics (headcount, industry, revenue) — the B2B version of persona targeting. But firmographics don't predict purchase, any more than "soccer mom in Utah, high income" predicts a Range Rover. Companies buy based on the **jobs/workflows they need done.** Anchor the ICP on (1) the workflow your product supports, or (2) the category a buyer would already be shopping in — not on demographics.

## 7. Pigeonholing is positioning — the niche math

"We don't want to pigeonhole ourselves" is a misunderstanding of positioning. Positioning means owning a place in *your customer's* mind, not everyone's. Niches are far bigger than they feel.

**The math (customers needed for $100M revenue):** $1K ACV → 100,000 · $10K ACV → 10,000 · $100K ACV → 1,000. With 8B people and ~400M companies, a tiny slice still builds a nine-figure business.

**Axes to narrow on (pick one):** one persona · one ecosystem · one use case · one industry · one attribute. (Every cloud-storage giant won one: Box=enterprise, iCloud=Apple users, Backblaze=unlimited backup, Wasabi=lowest cost.)

## 8. The 5-second hero test

People must understand *what* your product is before they'll consider buying it — yet most B2B sites never answer that in the first scroll, hiding behind "platform." A clear hero answers at least one of two grounding questions in ~5 seconds:

1. **"Which of my tools does this replace?"** → grounds the viewer in a known *product category*.
2. **"Which tasks in my job does this help me with?"** → grounds them in a known *job-to-be-done*.

(These map exactly to the two sides of the maturity fork, #1.) Establish the "what" first; *then* talk value. Outcome language + logos alone fail the test. Being known for 1–2 things is a strength — Salesforce=CRM, Gong=call recording.

**Headline length:** buy clarity even at the cost of length. The H1 is the one line guaranteed to be read. Lead the H1 with the JTBD; use the H2 for process + differentiation + a credibility marker. (Stripe moved from "Financial infrastructure to grow your revenue" to the longer, clearer "Accept payments, offer financial services, and build custom revenue models…")

## 9. Positioning inside a category — the 3 mistakes

If you enter a known category, avoid:
1. **Differentiation that isn't instantly apparent** — the moment you name the category, buyers compare you to the incumbent default. Give a clear reason to pick you over the multi-billion-dollar option; don't pitch table stakes as if you invented them.
2. **Coining a new name to stand out** — a fresh label just moves you to a shelf nobody knows. **Add a modifier to something known** instead ("smart" + "phone," not "pocket computer"). (DocuSign → "Intelligent Agreement Management" moved to an unknown shelf.)
3. **Living on multiple shelves** — subdividing across categories creates confusion and kills word-of-mouth. Only viable with the GTM muscle to win several markets at once.

## 10. One message, two proofs — the politician's structure

Most teams overcomplicate the message, chasing everyone with 10+ ideas until it's a "collective mush" no one can remember. Win like a disciplined campaign: **one main argument, repeated everywhere, backed by two specific, memorable supporting points.** (Mamdani: "make the city affordable" + "free buses" + "free childcare.") Simplicity is what makes a message repeatable — and repetition by others is how you win the mental slot.

## 11. The real buyer, not the C-suite

A very common failure: a homepage aimed at people who never visit it — the C-suite, adjacent stakeholders, or authority-less end users. The **real buyer** is the person who (1) champions the problem, (2) checks out the site, and (3) books the demo. Speak to *their* job-level concern ("we solve the problem you have"), not a boardroom outcome ("we increase revenue") — they won't say it to their boss, but they don't care about abstract revenue. Save challenger/decision-maker messaging for the sales process and sub-pages.

## 12. The 6-slide alignment deck

A positioning deck exists to align the whole company — and long decks defeat that because no one internalizes 50 slides (PMMs over-deliver to justify the role). Keep it to ~6 slides that align everyone on four things: **(1) what the company is, (2) what problem it solves, (3) how it solves it, (4) what specific aspects make the solution believable.** Like a campaign: a few headline themes everyone must remember; minor details can be phrased differently by different people.

## 13. The founder delusion gap

What sinks founders isn't what they don't know — it's what they're *certain of* that isn't true. Positioning is less strategy/tactics than **brutal honesty: seeing the business exactly as it is.** Pressure-test each belief against likely reality:

- "We've nailed our ICP" → *actually: "any B2B company with a budget."*
- "We validated the market" → *asked three founder friends.*
- "We have PMF" → *every deal needs a custom demo and new features.*
- "We're laser-focused" → *homepage says "for SMBs, enterprises, and everyone in between."*
- "Our product is 10× better" → *remove the logo and you couldn't tell it apart.*

Then get clear on four realities: the market you're *actually* in, who you *actually* compete with, what you *actually* do better, and your real resource limits. (This is the backbone of the ICP/champion personas in [panel.md](panel.md).)

## 14. The strategic-narrative trap

Big-change-in-the-world narratives ("the way we work is broken," "data is the new oil") aren't unique — unrelated categories all claim them, so they do nothing to help a buyer decide "is this for me?" The generic structure (Big Change → Winners & Losers → Promised Land) is interchangeable across dozens of companies. Buyers still need: what you are, what it does, what problem it solves, how you're different. **An original point of view *is* differentiation — but a big-change narrative is not an original point of view.** (Exception: it can land when pitching a CEO, who is abstract enough for it to work — not a scalable motion.)

## 15. Curse of knowledge — answer, then stop

Technical founders answer "what is your product?" like an engineer answers "2+2" — with a full derivation instead of "4." They think they're educating; they're confusing. When asked what the product is, deliver exactly three things and **stop:** (1) what it is, (2) who it's for, (3) when I'd use it. Cut the how-it-works derivation, the feature list, and the market-landscape monologue.

## 16. Product scope vs. brand equity

The bigger the product, the higher the *perceived risk* to a buyer — so "just keep shipping" quietly makes adoption harder. Incumbents (Salesforce) sell sprawling platforms because they've banked enormous **brand trust**; buyers accept long implementations on faith. A new startup has near-zero trust, so scope flips from asset to liability the moment you leave the founder's network (founder-led sales works because the buyer trusts the *founder*). Copying an incumbent's vague copy fails — their marketing only has to say "don't forget we exist"; yours must build trust from scratch, which often means **shrinking the product in the customer's mind.**

Related: **most positioning problems are caused by undisciplined product strategy.** Adding features is fine when they fit one coherent category; bolting on modules per user type and calling it a "platform" creates confusion and can leave you with a product no single buyer wants all of. Test: does one buyer want all of this at once? If not, don't sell it all at once.

## 17. You can't A/B test positioning

For a sales-led B2B startup, genuinely measuring positioning against revenue takes ~2 years (roll out messaging → update enablement → run campaigns → fill pipeline → track KPIs over 12–24 months). Execs want data at the *start*; the data only exists at the *end*. A reflexive "let's A/B test it / validate it" is usually **a lack of conviction disguised as rigor** — and it poisons the team. Positioning is one cohesive narrative carried across a 5–8 month sales cycle (homepage → champion → buying committee → business case), not a button color. If you're second-guessing, be honest about whether anything realistic could make you a believer — don't dress doubt as "data-driven." **Because you can't test it, you choose between directions on thesis and risk instead** — that's what the options map in [templates.md](templates.md#3-the-anchor--value-options-map) is for.

## 18. Positioning as a timeline (GTM rollout)

Positioning isn't a one-time homepage exercise — it's a single narrative sequenced across the whole go-to-market over months. The same anchor + use-case message repeats *verbatim* everywhere:

1. **Month 1 — document it on the homepage.** The hero is the canonical artifact; everything else inherits from it.
2. **Months 2–4 — push the identical message across channels.** LinkedIn outreach, cold email, ads, podcast, trade shows — all carrying the same alternative + use-case frame. *(Cold email subject that names the incumbent: "Annoyed with [incumbent]?"; ad: "The [incumbent] alternative you've been waiting for.")*
3. **Months 5–8 — enter through a non-executive champion.** ~87% of sales processes at $50M+ ARR companies start with a manager or director, not the C-suite (Wynter, 300 B2B companies) — and the bigger the company, the higher that share. The champion opens the door.
4. **Then — win the buying committee.** General Counsel, CISO, VP Finance, Head of Audit — the positioning has to survive translation to each. Co-create the business case → closed won.

Implication: positioning is validated over the sales cycle, not in an afternoon (ties to #17). When the user wants it, [create.md](create.md) can extend the Canvas message into this channel-by-channel rollout.
