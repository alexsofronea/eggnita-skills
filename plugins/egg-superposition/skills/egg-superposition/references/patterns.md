## The 15 failure modes

What breaks positioning, ranked by how often it showed up across the cases. An audit is largely: *which of these is present, and how badly.*

1. **Too broad** — speaks to many audiences/segments/use-cases at once; no wedge. The single most common failure.
2. **Vague** — a stranger can't tell what it is or does in ~5 seconds.
3. **No problem stated** — never names the pain, so the visitor can't tell if they're in the right place.
4. **No differentiation** — nothing says why you over the alternative; copy any competitor could run.
5. **No named competitive alternative** — doesn't anchor against an incumbent, a DIY build, or the status-quo way.
6. **Benefit without the "how"** — leads with outcomes/stats but never the mechanism, so claims aren't believable.
7. **Feature overload** — a wall of features, not scannable, the important thing buried.
8. **Jargon** — category language or "platform"-speak the buyer doesn't use or understand.
9. **Product-centric, not customer-centric** — written from the product's point of view, not the customer's world.
10. **Vision/investor-oriented** — wins VCs, loses customers; abstraction over concreteness.
11. **Unclear/ wrong buyer** — no defined audience, no problem owner, or aimed at the C-suite instead of the real buyer.
12. **Wrong or missing category** — no mental box for the buyer, or a category that's too small, unknown, or self-coined.
13. **Clever but not clear** — cleverness/slogan chosen over clarity.
14. **Buried lead** — the "wow" capability or the value proposition sits several scrolls down.
15. **Menu overload** — a grid of packages/tiers/options with no hierarchy; the buyer can't tell what to buy.

## The scoring dimensions

Audit rates each; create must satisfy each. Score pass / weak / fail with a one-line reason (quote the artifact) and the fix.

| Dimension | The question it answers | Fails when… |
|-----------|------------------------|-------------|
| **Clarity (5-second test)** | Can a stranger tell what this is in ~5s? | Needs a second read; hides behind "platform." |
| **Customer-centricity** | Does it lead with the reader's problem/world? | Leads with features, the company, or the founder's vision. |
| **Category** | Is it clear what *kind* of thing this is? | Buyer can't place it; category self-coined or unknown. |
| **Differentiation** | Why this over the alternatives (incl. doing nothing)? | Generic claims; no named alternative. |
| **Problem** | Is the pain named, in the buyer's words? | No problem section; visitor can't self-qualify. |
| **The "how"** | Is the mechanism shown? | Outcomes/stats float with no explanation of how. |
| **Value** | Is the payoff concrete and desirable? | Abstract benefit-speak, no tangible outcome. |
| **Focus** | One audience, one wedge? | Speaks to many segments/use-cases at once. |
| **Self-qualification** | Can the right buyer recognize themselves (and the wrong one leave)? | One-size messaging; no "this is for you if…". |
| **Proof** | Is the claim backed, and placed beside it? | Assertions with no evidence, or floated far from the claim. |
| **Plainness** | Would the buyer say these words out loud? | Insider jargon, buzzwords, vague abstractions. |
| **Scannability / single CTA** | Skimmable, with one obvious next step? | Wall of text; competing or missing CTAs. |

## The homepage anatomy

The section order a customer-centric B2B homepage tends to follow. Presence and weight flex by business type (see overlays); the *jobs* are stable. Each links to its copy techniques in [exemplars.md](exemplars.md).

1. **Hero** — in one glance: what it is, who it's for, the problem it kills. Must pass the 5-second test (answer "which tool does this replace?" *or* "which task does this help me do?"). The one line guaranteed to be read.
2. **Social proof bar** — customer logos immediately under the hero; borrow trust before asking for attention.
3. **Problem section** — name and agitate the pain in the buyer's language; make the status-quo cost felt. This is where the visitor decides "I'm in the right place."
4. **Positioning / category line** — "[Product] is a [category] built specifically for [use case]." Gives the buyer a mental box.
5. **Capability / how-it-works blocks** — verb-led headers matched to the named pains, each *showing the mechanism* (real UI), not just asserting a benefit.
6. **Differentiation vs the named alternative** — a comparison table ("Without X / With Y") or an "only X that Y" claim against the incumbent, DIY, or status quo.
7. **Packaging / "what you get"** — the offer made concrete: deliverables grid, plans, or scope. One clear offer, not a menu.
8. **Self-qualification** — "is this you?" / "this is for you if…" / "you've tried X but…". Lets the right buyer in and the wrong one out.
9. **Proof in context** — result stats tagged to named customers, testimonials placed beside the claim they back.
10. **Process / next step** — how the engagement or product works end to end, then one clear CTA.

## Business-type overlays

The anatomy shifts by what's being positioned.

- **SaaS / product** — the main battle is feature→outcome translation and showing the mechanism. Name the category or the incumbent; move floating stats next to the capability that earns them.
- **Services / consultancy** — package the intangible: a deliverables grid, transparent process/timeline, and a credibility anchor (where you did the work). Collapse a menu of tiers into one clear offer. Self-qualification matters more (you're also filtering fit). **The live Fletch homepage is the reference build** — see the consultancy composition in [exemplars.md](exemplars.md#consultancy).
- **Dev tools** — technical product, but frame the problem, not just the API. If the person who *feels the pain* is non-technical (e.g. a sales buyer for a technical tool), write in their language; technical accuracy they can't parse is a conversion leak. Proof skews to adoption.
- **Personal / bio** — one person as the product; the wedge is a point of view or a track record ("I built the playbook at [known company]"). One hero offer; demote the rest to "other ways I can help."

## How the pieces connect

- **Discovery** ([discovery.md](discovery.md)) feeds the strategic decisions in [frameworks.md](frameworks.md) — which market, which competition, which buyer, which problem.
- Those decisions produce the **brief**, which the **anatomy** above turns into a page, using the copy techniques in [exemplars.md](exemplars.md).
- **Audit** runs the failure modes + scoring dimensions over an existing page; when the failure is strategic (wrong buyer, no wedge), it kicks back to frameworks, not copy.
